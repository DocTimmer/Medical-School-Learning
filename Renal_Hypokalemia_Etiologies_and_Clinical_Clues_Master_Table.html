The Breakdown: Mastering Potassium Losses

1. The Transcellular Shift (Hiding the Potassium)

Sometimes the potassium isn't actually missing from the body; it's just hiding inside the cells. Insulin and β2-agonists (like Albuterol) actively stimulate the Na+/K+ ATPase pump, shoving potassium into the intracellular space. Furthermore, in an Alkalotic state, cells will trade hydrogen ions (H+) out into the blood to fix the pH, and in exchange, they pull potassium (K+) inside.

2. Renal Wasting (The Kidney Drain)

The kidney dictates potassium levels primarily via Aldosterone. Any state of hyperaldosteronism (or a mimic like Liddle syndrome) causes the collecting ducts to frantically absorb sodium and dump potassium into the urine. Diuretics like Loops and Thiazides (or their genetic equivalents, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes) increase the flow of fluid and sodium to the distal tubule, which mechanically forces potassium secretion.

3. Gastrointestinal Losses

Diarrhea and laxative abuse result in direct loss of potassium in the stool. Vomiting is a bit trickier—while stomach acid contains some potassium, the majority of the hypokalemia from vomiting is actually renal. Vomiting causes severe volume depletion, which turns on RAAS. The resulting spike in Aldosterone forces the kidneys to waste potassium in an attempt to save sodium and water.

4. Decreased Intake

The kidneys are generally very good at holding onto potassium if you aren't eating enough of it. Therefore, poor oral intake (like in malnutrition or severe alcoholism) rarely causes dangerous hypokalemia on its own, but it heavily exacerbates the condition if the patient is simultaneously losing potassium via the GI tract or kidneys.

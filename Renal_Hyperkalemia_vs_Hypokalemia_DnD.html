The Breakdown: Mastering Potassium Dynamics

1. The Shift (Intracellular vs. Extracellular)

Potassium lives primarily inside the cells. The body uses the Na+/K+ ATPase pump to keep it there. Insulin and β2-agonists (like Albuterol) rev up this pump, shoving potassium into the cell and causing Hypokalemia.

Conversely, a lack of insulin (Insulin deficiency/DKA) leaves potassium trapped outside. Furthermore, the pH dictates potassium movement: Acidosis pulls potassium out of the cell (Hyperkalemia), while Alkalosis pushes potassium into the cell (Hypokalemia) in exchange for hydrogen ions.

2. The Kidney Gatekeeper (Aldosterone)

The kidneys filter out potassium, and Aldosterone is the boss that tells the kidneys to dump it into the urine. If the kidneys fail (AKI/CKD) or the RAAS system is blocked (ACE-Inhibitors, ARBs, Hypoaldosteronism), potassium backs up into the blood, causing deadly Hyperkalemia.

On the flip side, states of Hyperaldosteronism or the use of heavy diuretics (Loops, Thiazides) force the kidneys to aggressively waste potassium, leading to Hypokalemia.

3. The Spill vs. The Drain

Hyperkalemia (The Spill): Because cells are bags of potassium, massive cellular destruction (Crush trauma / Rhabdomyolysis / Tumor Lysis Syndrome) causes all that intracellular potassium to violently spill into the bloodstream.

Hypokalemia (The Drain): You lose potassium from the GI tract. While diarrhea is a direct loss of potassium, vomiting is tricky. Vomiting loses stomach acid (causing alkalosis) and volume. The volume drop triggers Aldosterone, which forces the kidneys to waste potassium. Thus, vomiting causes hypokalemia mostly through renal wasting.

4. The Illusion (Pseudohyperkalemia)

Always trust the patient over the lab! If a blood draw was difficult, the red blood cells may have hemolyzed (popped) inside the test tube, spilling their potassium into the sample. If the lab reads 7.5 but the patient's ECG is normal, redraw the lab!

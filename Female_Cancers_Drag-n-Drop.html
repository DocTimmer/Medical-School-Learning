Germ CellDysgerminoma Adolescent / young female (10–30 y/o); painless adnexal mass; ↑ LDH, ↑ PLAP, normal AFP, +/- mildly ↑ hCG; associated with Turner syndrome / gonadal dysgenesis; highly radiosensitive. Sheets of uniform "fried-egg" cells (clear cytoplasm, central nuclei) separated by fibrous pseudolobules containing a lymphocytic infiltrate. US → Unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (preserves fertility); exceptionally responsive to chemotherapy if advanced.
Germ CellYolk Sac Tumor (Endodermal Sinus) Young female (<30 y/o) w/ rapidly expanding, aggressive pelvic mass + abdominal pain; markedly ↑ AFP, normal hCG. Schiller-Duval bodies (glomeruloid-like structures with a central vessel) and intracellular/extracellular hyaline droplets. Surgical resection (fertility-sparing oophorectomy) + adjuvant BEP chemotherapy (Bleomycin, Etoposide, Cisplatin); highly aggressive.
Germ CellMature Cystic Teratoma (Dermoid Cyst) Reproductive-age female (20–30 y/o); often asymptomatic or presents with acute pain from ovarian torsion; pelvic X-ray or CT shows calcifications/fat densities; Struma ovarii variant causes hyperthyroidism. Multiple mature germ layers (calcifications, teeth, hair, sebum). Malignant transformation is rare but typically presents as squamous cell carcinoma. Cystectomy or oophorectomy; benign in females.
Germ CellImmature Teratoma Adolescent / young girl (~18 y/o); fast-growing malignant mass; mixed with mature elements (cartilage, bone). Contains immature/embryonic neural tissue (neuroectoderm, primitive neuroepithelial tubules). Graded based on amount of immature neuroepithelium. Oophorectomy + combo chemotherapy (except for Stage I, Grade 1 which can undergo surveillance).
Germ CellChoriocarcinoma Young female; can be gestational or non-gestational; highly malignant with early hematogenous spread to lungs (hemoptysis / "cannonball" lesions); ↑↑↑ [β-hCG]; mimics hyperthyroidism/precocious puberty due to hCG-TSH/LH homology. Cytotrophoblasts and syncytiotrophoblasts with extensive hemorrhage & necrosis; lacks villi. Systemic chemotherapy (Methotrexate-based for gestational; cisplatin-based BEP for non-gestational) +/- surgical resection.
Germ CellEmbryonal Carcinoma Adolescent / young female (~15 y/o); rare, highly aggressive; ↑ AFP and ↑ hCG; strongly CD30 positive. Sheets of primitive, pleomorphic cells with indistinct borders, extensive sheet-like necrosis, and hemorrhage. Surgical resection + combo chemotherapy; hematogenous spread is common.
Sex Cord-StromalAdult Granulosa Cell Tumor Peri- or Postmenopausal female (~50–54 y/o); presents with abnormal/postmenopausal uterine bleeding or breast tenderness driven by ↑ estrogen; large, unilateral mass; ↑ Inhibin (A/B) and ↑ AMH. Call-Exner bodies (microfollicular fluid-filled spaces resembling follicles) and "coffee-bean" nuclei (longitudinal nuclear grooves). Driven by a FOXL2 gene mutation. Diagnostic: Pelvic US → Endometrial biopsy (to rule out concurrent endometrial carcinoma).Treatment: TAH-BSO. Characterized by late recurrence (10–20 years later).
Sex Cord-StromalJuvenile Granulosa Cell Tumor Young girl / Pre-pubertal child (<20 y/o) presenting with precocious puberty (early breast development, pubic hair, vaginal bleeding) or severe menorrhagia; rapidly growing mass; ↑ Inhibin. Sheets of pleomorphic cells with a high mitotic index; Call-Exner bodies and "coffee-bean" grooves are absent / rare. Associated with trisomy 12. Treatment: Unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy to preserve fertility (if localized to one ovary). More aggressive early clinical course.
Sex Cord-StromalSertoli-Leydig Cell Tumor Young female (20–30 y/o) presenting with rapid-onset virilization / defeminization driven by ↑ testosterone (amenorrhea, breast atrophy, hirsutism, clitomegaly, deep voice). Tubular structures (Sertoli cells) mixed with interstitial cells containing pink cytoplasm (Leydig cells); may feature Reinke crystalloids (rectangular, eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions). Associated with DICER1 mutations. Diagnostic: Ultrasound may reveal a hidden/very small solid adnexal lesion.Treatment: Unilateral oophorectomy. (Voice changes and clitomegaly may partially persist).
Sex Cord-StromalThecoma Postmenopausal female; benign neoplasm; produces estrogen leading to postmenopausal bleeding due to endometrial hyperplasia; often paired with fibromas (Fibrothecoma). Plump spindle cells with lipid droplets. Treatment: Surgical excision (oophorectomy) is curative; virtually always benign.
Sex Cord-StromalFibroma Middle-aged to older female (~50 y/o); hormonally inactive; classic vignette pairs this tumor with Meigs Syndrome triad: Ovarian Fibroma + Ascites + Right-sided Hydrothorax. Composed of bundles of benign spindle-shaped fibroblasts. Treatment: Surgical excision is curative. The ascites and pleural effusion completely resolve after the tumor is removed.
Epithelial OvarianSerous Cystadenoma Premenopausal / Reproductive-age female (30–40 y/o); often asymptomatic or mild pelvic pressure; pelvic US shows a thin-walled, unilocular cystic mass filled with clear, watery fluid. Lined by a single layer of ciliated columnar epithelium resembling fallopian tube epithelium; no nuclear atypia, no stromal invasion, no papillary complexity. Conservative surgical management: Ovarian cystectomy or unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (fertility-sparing).
Epithelial OvarianSerous Cystadenocarcinoma Postmenopausal female (60–70 y/o) with abdominal distention/bloating, early satiety, or ascites; most common malignant ovarian tumor overall; frequently bilateral (~66%); ↑ CA-125; associated with BRCA1/2 and Lynch syndrome. Papillary architecture, cellular pleomorphism, destructive stromal invasion, and concentric laminated calcifications called Psammoma bodies. Often originates from the fimbriated end of the fallopian tube. Diagnostic: Pelvic US → staging laparotomy.Treatment: Surgical debulking (tumor cytoreduction) + platinum-taxane chemotherapy (Cisplatin/Carboplatin + Paclitaxel).
Epithelial OvarianMucinous Cystadenoma Reproductive-age female (30–50 y/o); presents with a remarkably massive, slow-growing abdominal mass causing pronounced abdominal fullness or distention. Multilocular mass filled with thick, gelatinous/mucinous fluid; lined by a single layer of tall columnar cells with apical mucin vacuoles resembling endocervix / GI epithelium; no stromal invasion. Surgical resection: Oophorectomy or cystectomy with careful intraoperative intact removal to avoid spillage of mucinous contents.
Epithelial OvarianMucinous Cystadenocarcinoma Postmenopausal female (50–60 y/o); rapidly expanding pelvic/abdominal mass with early peritoneal dissemination; rupture/seeding results in Pseudomyxoma peritonei (massive intra-abdominal gelatinous ascites / "jelly belly"). Marked cellular atypia and destructive stromal invasion of mucin-producing epithelial cells. Treatment: Surgical debulking + systemic chemotherapy. High-Yield Note: Must rule out an appendiceal primary neoplasm (or GI tract metastasis) with a colonoscopy/appendectomy.
Epithelial OvarianEndometrioid Carcinoma Perimenopausal or postmenopausal female (50–60 y/o); frequently arises on a background of chronic ovarian endometriosis (alongside endometriomas); associated with PTEN mutations and Lynch syndrome. Malignant glandular proliferation containing tubular glands that mimic proliferative/secretory endometrium. Treatment: TAH-BSO + surgical staging. High-Yield Association: 15–30% of cases are diagnosed with a synchronous (concurrent) primary endometrial adenocarcinoma of the uterus.
Epithelial OvarianClear Cell Carcinoma Middle-aged to older female (40–60 y/o); rare, aggressive epithelial subtype presenting with a large unilateral pelvic mass; strongly associated with pre-existing pelvic endometriosis. Features "hobnail" cells (prominent atypical nuclei protruding into gland lumens) and glycogen-rich clear cytoplasm. Treatment: Surgical debulking + aggressive chemotherapy; frequently demonstrates resistance to standard platinum agents. May trigger paraneoplastic hypercalcemia.
Epithelial OvarianBrenner Tumor Postmenopausal female (~50–70 y/o); almost always benign, unilateral, and found incidentally as a solid, pale-yellow/tan, circumscribed, fibrous ovarian mass. Nests of urothelial/transitional epithelium (resembling the urinary bladder) surrounded by dense ovarian stroma; characteristically exhibits "coffee-bean" nuclei (longitudinal nuclear grooves). Treatment: Simple surgical excision (oophorectomy/cystectomy) is curative. "B's": Brenner = Benign, Bbladder-like epithelium, Becoming encapsulated, with "coffee-Bean" nuclei.
Non-Neoplastic CystsFollicular Cyst (Younger / Pre-pubertal) Young girl (<8 y/o) presenting with sudden-onset peripheral precocious puberty (breast buds, pubic hair, early menses) due to autonomous ↑ estrogen; bone age may be advanced; low/suppressed LH/FSH. Arises when a follicle fails to rupture or an unruptured follicle undergoes fluid distention; lined by granulosa and outerca cells without atypia. Next Step: Pelvic ultrasound (shows unilocular, thin-walled simple cyst). Treatment: Reassurance and observation; most spontaneously regress over 2–3 months.
Non-Neoplastic CystsFollicular Cyst (Reproductive-Age) Reproductive-age female presenting with a sudden delay in menses followed by heavy, irregular bleeding (menorrhagia/anovulatory bleeding); may cause mild unilateral pelvic aching. Arises when a dominant graafian follicle fails to rupture and continues growing; lined by normal granulosa cells. Next Step: Pelvic ultrasound to confirm a simple cyst (<5–10 cm). Treatment: Expectant surveillance for 6–8 weeks as most self-resolve.
Non-Neoplastic CystsCorpus Luteum Cyst Reproductive-age female presenting with delayed menses, a palpable adnexal mass, and unilateral lower quadrant pain during the luteal phase (days 14–28); can rupture causing sudden pain and hemoperitoneum. Develops from a failure of the corpus luteum to regress after ovulation; central hemorrhage surrounded by a rim of luteinized granulosa cells. Next Step: Pelvic US shows thick-walled cyst with peripheral "ring of fire" vascularity. Always obtain a β-hCG to rule out ectopic pregnancy. Treatment: Observation if stable; laparoscopy if unstable.
Non-Neoplastic CystsTheca Lutein Cyst Pregnant female or fertility patient; characteristically bilateral and massive; driven by abnormally high levels of hCG; associated with Choriocarcinoma, Complete Hydatidiform Mole, or twin pregnancies. Hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the theca interna cells driven by massive gonadotropin (hCG) overstimulation. Next Step: Quantitative β-hCG and pelvic US (shows multi-cystic, "soap-bubble"/"spoke-wheel" bilateral ovaries). Treatment: Conservative observation. Do NOT resect surgically; they spontaneously regress when hCG drops.
Non-Neoplastic CystsEndometrioma (Chocolate Cyst) Reproductive-age female (20–40 y/o) presenting with the classic triad of Dysmenorrhea, Dyspareunia, and Dyschezia, alongside chronic pelvic pain and infertility; US shows homogeneous, low-level internal echoes ("ground-glass"). Ectopic endometrial tissue outside the uterus undergoing cyclic hemorrhage; cyst is filled with thick, dark-brown altered old blood ("chocolate fluid"). Next Step: Pelvic ultrasound. Laparoscopy/biopsy for definitive diagnosis. Treatment: First-line medical (NSAIDs, continuous OCPs, GnRH agonists). Surgical cystectomy if symptomatic/large.
Non-Neoplastic CystsPolycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) Adolescent / young reproductive-age female with oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea, infertility, and signs of hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, male baldness); associated with obesity, insulin resistance, acanthosis nigricans. ↑ LH to FSH ratio (>3:1); increased androgen production by theca cells. Long-term unopposed estrogen increases risk of Endometrial Hyperplasia and Adenocarcinoma. Next Step: Pelvic US showing enlarged ovaries with >12 small subependymal follicles arranged peripherally ("string of pearls"). Treatment: Weight loss (first-line); OCPs; Metformin; Clomiphene/Letrozole for fertility.
Cervical & UterineCervical Cancer (Squamous vs. Adeno) Female w/ post-coital or postmenopausal bleeding; malodorous watery discharge; history of smoking, HIV/immunosuppression, or lack of regular Pap screening; speculum exam shows a friable, exophytic mass; advanced disease causes lateral invasion leading to ureteral obstruction / hydronephrosis. Koilocytic atypia (cells with hyperchromatic, wrinkled nuclei surrounded by a clear halo) indicates precursor HPV 16/18 infection. Squamous cell carcinoma arises from ectocervix/transformation zone; adenocarcinoma arises from endocervix. Diagnostic: Pap smear/HPV co-test → Colposcopy with biopsy.Treatment: Localized = Radical hysterectomy or cisplatin-based chemoradiation.
Cervical & UterineType I Endometrioid Adenocarcinoma (Younger) Younger female (30–50 y/o) with chronic anovulatory cycles (PCOS); presenting with severe AUB / menorrhagia; comorbid with metabolic syndrome, severe obesity, type 2 diabetes. Chronic unopposed estrogen stimulation driving a progression from atypical endometrial hyperplasia to invasive carcinoma. Driven by PTEN loss (mutations) or KRAS/MSI mutations. Next Step: TVUS showing an endometrial stripe >4 mm (or focal thickening) → Endometrial Biopsy (EMB). Treatment: Progestin therapy temporarily if fertility preservation is desired; otherwise, hysterectomy.
Cervical & UterineType I Endometrioid Adenocarcinoma (Older) Postmenopausal female (55–65 y/o) presenting with classic painless postmenopausal bleeding; risk factors include obesity (peripheral conversion to estrone), early menarche, nulliparity, or Tamoxifen use; associated with Lynch Syndrome. Back-to-back neoplastic columnar glands lacking intervening stroma; invades the myometrium. Next Step: Any postmenopausal bleeding must be evaluated with Endometrial Biopsy (EMB) or D&C. Treatment: Total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH-BSO) + pelvic lymphadenectomy.
Cervical & UterineType II Non-Endometrioid Carcinoma (Serous/Clear Cell) Elderly, thin postmenopausal female (70–80 y/o) presenting with postmenopausal bleeding or abnormal discharge; unrelated to estrogen exposure or obesity; arises from atrophic endometrium; poor prognosis. Serous type: Papillary architecture with cellular tufting, marked nuclear atypia, and Psammoma bodies. Heavily associated with p53 mutations (mutant p53 immunostaining on biopsy). Next Step: Endometrial biopsy / D&C. Treatment: Highly aggressive; treated like ovarian cancer with TAH-BSO + surgical staging/omentecomy + systemic platinum-taxane chemotherapy.
Cervical & UterineUterine Leiomyosarcoma Postmenopausal female (50–60 y/o) presenting with rapid uterine enlargement, pelvic pain, and postmenopausal bleeding; more common in African American patients; metastasizes hematogenously (lungs). Arises de novo from smooth muscle (NOT from a pre-existing fibroid). Bulky, fleshy mass with necrosis, hemorrhage, marked cellular atypia, and a high mitotic index (>10 mitoses per 10 HPF). Next Step: Pelvic MRI or biopsy. (Often diagnosed incidentally after hysterectomy for presumed benign fibroids). Treatment: Total hysterectomy. Does not respond well to hormonal therapies.
Cervical & UterineUterine Leiomyoma (Symptomatic Bleeding) Reproductive-age female (30–40 y/o), often of African American descent (highest risk), presenting with menorrhagia (heavy, prolonged bleeding), severe pelvic pressure, or back pain; asymmetrically enlarged, firm, non-tender, irregular ("bumpy") uterus. Monoclonal proliferation of benign smooth muscle cells arranged in a classic whorled pattern with well-demarcated margins; no cellular atypia or necrosis. Estrogen/progesterone sensitive. Next Step: Pelvic ultrasound (shows well-circumscribed, hypoechoic uterine masses). Treatment: OCPs, progestin IUD, or Leuprolide (GnRH agonist) to shrink tumors. Myomectomy (fertility) or Hysterectomy (fertility complete).
Cervical & UterineUterine Leiomyoma (Infertility / Obstetric) Reproductive-age female presenting with recurrent pregnancy loss or infertility (due to submucosal or intramural fibroids distorting the cavity); OR pregnant patient with sudden localized pain due to "red degeneration". Red degeneration: Hemorrhagic necrosis of a fibroid outgrowing its blood supply during pregnancy. Submucosal type distorts the endometrial cavity and disrupts embryo implantation. Next Step: Pelvic US, Saline-infusion sonohysterography (SIS), or Hysteroscopy.Treatment: Hysteroscopic myomectomy to resect submucosal fibroids to restore fertility. For red degeneration in pregnancy: conservative NSAIDs/analgesics only.
Breast BenignFibrocystic Changes Premenopausal female (20–50 y/o, peak 30–50) with bilateral, multifocal breast pain (cyclic mastalgia) and diffuse "lumpy-bumpy" irregularity that worsen premenstrually and improve after menses; intermittent green-brown nipple discharge; mammogram shows heterogeneous, dense parenchyma. Nonproliferative change showing cystic dilation of terminal duct lobular units (TDLUs) (grossly "blue-dome" cysts), papillary apocrine metaplasia, and stromal fibrosis. No increased risk of breast cancer. Next Step: Re-examination in 2–4 weeks (follicular phase after next menses) to confirm regression. Treatment: Reassurance; no treatment needed.
Breast BenignSimple Breast Cyst Premenopausal female (most frequently 30–50 y/o) with a solitary, smooth, mobile, tender mass (worsens before menses); ultrasound shows a round/oval, well-circumscribed anechoic (fluid-filled) mass with distinct borders and posterior acoustic enhancement. Fluid-filled duct dilation within the TDLU lined by flattened or apocrine-type epithelium without atypia. No increased risk of breast cancer. Treatment: Reassurance / no treatment needed if asymptomatic. If painful or patient is anxious: Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) is diagnostic & therapeutic (clear, nonbloody fluid with complete mass resolution).
Breast BenignUsual Ductal Hyperplasia (UDH) Female (30–60 y/o, mean 54 y/o); clinically asymptomatic with no specific mammographic abnormality; discovered incidentally on biopsy of another breast lesion. Intraductal proliferation of a heterogeneous (polymorphous) population of epithelial and myoepithelial cells forming irregular, slit-like peripheral lumens. Treatment: Benign proliferative lesion (slight ~1.5× risk); no treatment needed.
Breast BenignSclerosing Adenosis Female (30–55 y/o); clinically asymptomatic; detected on screening mammography as a mass with calcifications that radiographically mimics invasive breast carcinoma. Proliferative lesion within the TDLU showing an increased number of compressed, distorted acini surrounded by dense stromal fibrosis and calcifications. Slight (~1.5–2×) increased risk for cancer. Next Step: Core needle biopsy to distinguish from carcinoma. Treatment: Benign; no treatment needed.
Breast BenignAtypical Hyperplasia (ADH / ALH) Female (40–60 y/o); clinically asymptomatic; discovered on screening mammography showing clustered microcalcifications. Clonal intraductal (ADH) or lobular (ALH) proliferation of a monotonous (uniform) population of dysplastic cells partially filling the duct/lobule. Major risk factor (~4–5× ↑ risk) for invasive breast cancer. Management: Increased screening and possible surgical excision (to rule out adjacent DCIS/invasive cancer) +/- risk-reducing endocrine therapy (tamoxifen/raloxifene).
Breast BenignIntraductal Papilloma Premenopausal female (20–40 y/o) presenting with unilateral bloody or serosanguinous nipple discharge; most common cause of nipple discharge; usually no palpable mass and normal mammogram; ultrasound shows a solid nodule inside a single dilated subareolar duct. Small benign fibroepithelial tumor within a lactiferous duct composed of branching fibrovascular cores (papillary fronds) lined by both epithelial and myoepithelial cell layers. Slight ↑ risk for cancer. Next Step: Breast ultrasound + core needle biopsy (to rule out papillary carcinoma/DCIS). Treatment: Surgical excision of the involved duct.
Breast BenignFibroadenoma Young female (<35 y/o; 20–40 y/o); most common benign breast tumor in women <35; presents as a solitary, firm, rubbery, well-circumscribed, highly mobile, painless mass; grows during pregnancy and fluctuates with the menstrual cycle (estrogen-sensitive). Benign biphasic fibroepithelial tumor with a rounded contour composed of overgrowth of both fibrous stroma and glandular epithelium. No increased risk of breast cancer. Next Step: Breast ultrasound (shows well-defined homogeneous solid mass). Treatment: Observation; surgical excision for enlarging (>2–4 cm) or symptomatic lesions.
Breast BenignPhyllodes Tumor Female in 5th decade (40–50 y/o) presenting with a large (often >3–6 cm), solitary, well-circumscribed, firm, polylobulated mass with rapid growth over weeks to months. Biphasic fibroepithelial tumor arising from periductal connective tissue with characteristic "leaf-like" projections (lobulations) of hypercellular stroma lined by epithelium. Some may become malignant. Next Step: Mammography/US → Core needle biopsy (required to differentiate from fibroadenoma). Treatment: Surgical excision with wide margins.
Breast BenignFat Necrosis Female of any age following breast trauma (e.g., seatbelt injury in MVC or prior surgery; up to 50% may not report trauma); presents with breast pain and a firm, irregular mass (+/- ecchymosis or skin retraction mimicking cancer); mammography shows a calcified oil cyst (radiolucent center with peripheral calcified rim). Trauma damages adipocytes → release of cytoplasmic triglycerides and lipase → saponification of fat with calcium → biopsy shows necrotic fat, lipid-laden foamy macrophages (foam cells), and multinucleated giant cells. Next Step: Biopsy if mass mimics malignancy on imaging. Treatment: No treatment / reassurance; benign and usually resolves spontaneously.
Breast BenignMammary Duct Ectasia Perimenopausal female (45–55 y/o); associated with smoking; presents with areolar pain, thick multicolored nipple discharge, inverted nipple, or a periareolar mass; mammography shows dilated linear branching densities in the subareolar region. Dilation of subareolar lactiferous ducts filled with inspissated debris alongside periductal chronic inflammation and fibrosis. Treatment: Usually resolves spontaneously; symptomatic care with warm compresses and analgesics, antibiotics if secondary infection, or surgical excision if persistent.
Breast BenignLactational Mastitis, Breast Abscess & Galactocele Breastfeeding postpartum female: Mastitis = unilateral wedge-shaped erythema, tenderness, swelling + fever (>38°C); Abscess = persistent fever + fluctuant mass (+/- purplish skin necrosis); Galactocele = afebrile, mobile subareolar mass after stopping breastfeeding (US shows heterogeneous fat/water milk cyst). Mastitis / Abscess: Nipple fissures + milk stasis allow bacterial entry, most commonly Staphylococcus aureus. Galactocele: Non-infectious milk retention cyst caused by duct obstruction after weaning. Mastitis: Oral cephalexin or dicloxacillin + CONTINUE breastfeeding from both breasts. Abscess: Antibiotics + needle aspiration (or surgical incision & drainage if skin necrosis/large). Galactocele: Reassurance or aspiration.
Breast BenignGynecomastia (Physiologic vs. Pathologic) Male with firm, rubbery, tender subareolar glandular tissue bilaterally: Physiologic in newborns, puberty (Tanner 3), and elderly; Pathologic in cirrhosis, CKD, Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY; tall, small firm testes, 15–20× ↑ breast cancer risk), Leydig cell tumor, or drugs. Driven by ↑ estrogen relative to androgen activity: Newborn (placental maternal hormones), Puberty (temporary hormone imbalance), Elderly (androgen–estrogen mismatch). Drugs: Spironolactone, finasteride, leuprolide, ketoconazole, risperidone/antipsychotics (D2 blockade → ↑ prolactin). Management: Pubertal/Physiologic = reassurance. Pathologic = discontinue offending medication (e.g., stop spironolactone) and treat underlying disease (testosterone replacement in Klinefelter).
Breast CancerDuctal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS) Asymptomatic female detected early on screening mammography as clustered, pleomorphic or linear microcalcifications; usually does NOT produce a palpable mass; increases risk of invasive cancer in the same breast and quadrant. Neoplastic ductal epithelial cells fill the ductal lumen (e.g., comedo central necrosis) WITHOUT basement membrane penetration into surrounding stroma. Next Step: Stereotactic core needle biopsy + ER/PR testing. Treatment: Lumpectomy + radiation (or mastectomy) + adjuvant endocrine therapy if ER+.
Breast CancerLobular Carcinoma In Situ (LCIS) Premenopausal female; produces NO palpable mass and NO mammographic calcifications → discovered as an incidental biopsy finding; marker of ↑ risk of invasive carcinoma in EITHER breast (bilateral risk). Neoplastic proliferation of loosely cohesive cells within terminal duct lobules caused by ↓ E-cadherin expression; basement membrane remains intact. Management: Surveillance + risk-reducing endocrine therapy (tamoxifen or raloxifene); surgical excision if pleomorphic subtype.
Breast CancerInvasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC) Most common invasive breast cancer (~75–80%); presents with a firm, fibrous, "rock-hard," irregular, nonmobile mass in the upper outer quadrant; Cooper ligament infiltration → skin dimpling; mammogram shows a spiculated (stellate) mass with microcalcifications. Nests, cords, and tubules of malignant duct-like glandular cells that invade through the basement membrane into a dense desmoplastic stroma; tumor cells retain E-cadherin expression. Workup: Diagnostic mammogram + breast US → Needle core biopsy → Determine ER, PR, and HER2 status. #1 Prognostic Factor: Axillary lymph node status (and tumor size). Surgical Pearl: Axillary dissection can injure the long thoracic nerve → winged scapula (serratus anterior palsy).
Breast CancerInvasive Lobular Carcinoma (ILC) Postmenopausal or middle-aged female with an ill-defined breast mass/thickening; often bilateral and multifocal; typically ER-positive. Small, discohesive tumor cells infiltrating the stroma in orderly single-file lines ("Indian file") with no duct/tubule formation and minimal desmoplastic response due to loss of E-cadherin expression. Management: Diagnostic imaging + core biopsy + ER/PR/HER2 → surgical resection + endocrine/systemic therapy. (In pregnancy: Surgery + 2nd/3rd-trimester chemo [doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide/5-FU], stopped 3–4 wks before delivery).
Breast CancerInflammatory Breast Cancer Female (~50–55 y/o) with a rapidly enlarging, red, warm, swollen breast and diffuse thickened, pitted skin resembling an orange peel (peau d'orange); afebrile and not breastfeeding; usually lacks a discrete mass; often mistaken for mastitis; poor prognosis (50% 5-yr survival). Tumor cells infiltrating and obstructing the dermal lymphatics in the skin → localized lymphedema with skin dimpling/pitting around tethered hair follicles (peau d'orange). Management: Core needle + skin punch biopsy → aggressive trimodality therapy (neoadjuvant chemotherapy + modified radical mastectomy + radiation).
Breast CancerPaget Disease of the Breast Older female (~60 y/o) with a chronic, unilateral red, scaly, crusting, eczematous lesion of the nipple and areola that does NOT improve with topical corticosteroids; associated with an underlying DCIS or invasive carcinoma in 80–90% of cases. Extension of underlying ductal adenocarcinoma up the lactiferous ducts into the contiguous skin, showing intraepithelial adenocarcinoma cells (Paget cells) within the epidermis of the nipple. Next Step: Nipple biopsy → Mammography + breast US to identify the underlying in situ or invasive carcinoma (80–90%) → surgical resection.
Breast CancerHereditary & Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (BRCA1/2) Hereditary Clues: Young age (<45 y/o), 1st-degree relative diagnosed <50 y/o, triple-negative status (ER⊝/PR⊝/HER2⊝), male relative with breast cancer, breast + ovarian cancer in family, Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry. General Risk Factors: ↑ estrogen exposure (early menarche, nulliparity, postmenopausal obesity), atypical hyperplasia, alcohol, radiation. BRCA1/2 encode tumor suppressor proteins that repair double-stranded DNA breaks (homologous recombination). BRCA1 → triple-negative breast cancer & ovarian cancer; BRCA2 → male breast cancer. Other hereditary genes: PALB2, ATM, MSH6, PTEN, TP53. Triple-Negative Treatment: Chemotherapy (doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, paclitaxel) ± Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1 mAb that restores antitumor T-cell activity; causes immune-related colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis). No endocrine or HER2 therapy.
Breast CancerER/PR+ Endocrine Therapy (Tamoxifen, AIs & GnRH Agonists) Used for ER+ / PR+ breast cancer (nuclear staining on IHC): Tamoxifen (pre- & postmenopausal); Aromatase Inhibitors — Anastrozole, Letrozole, Exemestane (postmenopausal); GnRH Agonists — Leuprolide, Goserelin (premenopausal ovarian suppression). Tamoxifen (SERM): ER antagonist in breast, partial agonist in endometrium and agonist in bone (↑ BMD). AIs: Inhibit aromatase → ↓ peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens. Leuprolide/Goserelin: Continuous GnRH receptor stimulation → suppresses ovarian estrogen. Adverse Effects: Tamoxifen → Venous thromboembolism (VTE), endometrial carcinoma, & uterine leiomyosarcoma (evaluate bleeding with endometrial biopsy). Aromatase Inhibitors → ↓ bone mineral density / fractures (monitor DEXA) & vaginal dryness. Leuprolide/Goserelin → hot flashes, vaginal dryness, ↓ libido, ↓ BMD.
Breast CancerHER2+ Targeted Therapy & Chemotherapy Toxicities Trastuzumab (used for HER2 / Erb-B2+ tumors with membranous IHC staining); Doxorubicin, Cyclophosphamide, and Paclitaxel (backbone cytotoxic chemotherapy for triple-negative and high-risk invasive breast cancer). Trastuzumab: Anti-HER2 mAb → inhibits HER2 tyrosine kinase signaling & promotes immune destruction. Doxorubicin: Anthracycline (free-radical cardiotoxicity). Cyclophosphamide: DNA alkylating agent. Paclitaxel: Taxane (microtubule stabilization). Signature Toxicities: Trastuzumab → Cardiomyopathy (monitor echo). Doxorubicin → Dilated cardiomyopathy / ↓ LVEF (prevent with dexrazoxane). Cyclophosphamide → Hemorrhagic cystitis (dysuria + hematuria without bacteria). Paclitaxel → Stocking-glove peripheral neuropathy (↓ pinprick/vibration).

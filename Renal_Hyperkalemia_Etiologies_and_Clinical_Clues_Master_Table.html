The Breakdown: Mastering Potassium Shifts

1. The Illusion: Pseudohyperkalemia

Always trust the patient over the lab! If the lab says potassium is critically high (e.g., 7.5), but the patient feels fine and the ECG is completely normal, the red blood cells likely popped inside the test tube (hemolysis) during a difficult blood draw. Redraw the lab before treating.

2. The Shift: Intracellular vs. Extracellular

Potassium is mostly kept inside the cells. Insulin and beta-agonists push it in. However, when the blood becomes highly acidic (Acidosis) or hyperosmolar (DKA), potassium is forcefully dragged out of the cells and into the blood. The total body potassium might actually be low, but the blood level spikes dangerously.

3. The Spill: Cellular Destruction

Cells are essentially bags of potassium. When massive amounts of tissue are destroyed—whether from a crush injury (Rhabdomyolysis) or chemotherapy destroying cancer cells (Tumor Lysis Syndrome)—all that intracellular potassium violently spills into the bloodstream.

4. The Drain: Failure to Excrete

The kidneys are the ultimate regulators of potassium, driven heavily by Aldosterone. If the kidneys fail (AKI/CKD), they simply can't filter it out. If the Aldosterone pathway is blocked (by ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or Type IV RTA), the distal tubules refuse to secrete potassium into the urine. This is why giving a patient with CKD an ACE inhibitor requires close monitoring.
